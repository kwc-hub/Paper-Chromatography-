Method and theory

  1. Draw a horizontal pencil baseline about 2 cm from the bottom of chromatography paper. Place small, concentrated spots of the reference dyes and unknown on it.
  2. Put a shallow depth of solvent in a beaker, with the baseline above the liquid level. Cover the beaker to reduce solvent evaporation.
  3. The solvent rises through the paper. It is the mobile phase; the paper is the stationary phase.
  4. Each soluble substance distributes differently between these phases. A dye with relatively stronger attraction/solubility in the moving solvent travels farther; one with relatively stronger attraction to the paper travels less far. This separates a mixture into spots.
  5. Remove the paper before the front reaches the top. Immediately mark the solvent front in pencil; allow the chromatogram to dry.
  6. Measure vertically from the baseline to the centre of each spot and to the solvent front. Compare the unknown's \(R_f\) values and colours with reference substances run under the same conditions.

\(R_f=\dfrac{\text{distance moved by substance from baseline}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front from baseline}}\)

\(R_f\) has no unit and lies between 0 and 1. It is useful for identification only when the same solvent, paper and conditions are used; it is evidence that substances are likely the same, not absolute proof.

Analysis challenge

Exam-focused interpretation

  • One spot suggests a pure substance in the chosen solvent; two or more spots show a mixture of substances that have separated.
  • For a match, compare both the colour and the position/\(R_f\) of the unknown spot with standards on the same chromatogram.
  • Do not use pen for the baseline: its dyes may dissolve and separate. Do not immerse the sample spots: they may dissolve directly into the solvent rather than travel up the paper.
  • Keep spots small and dry between applications to improve separation. Marking the solvent front immediately matters because the wet edge disappears as the paper dries.
